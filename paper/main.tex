\begin{abstract}
When a brain-computer interface (BCI) errs, the user's electroencephalogram carries
an error-related potential (ErrP) the system can use to self-correct, but exploiting it
usually costs every new user tens of minutes of calibration. We ask whether
meta-learning can replace that calibration with four labeled trials, and answer with
a controlled benchmark on two structurally different ErrP paradigms under
leave-one-subject-out evaluation with subject-level statistics ($N{=}16$ each).
Gradient meta-learners and a FiLM-conditioned encoder on principal-component features
beat a from-scratch decoder by 5--7 points on one corpus and 15--16 on the other, and
the gain survives realistic calibration: with four consecutive, unbalanced trials as
a user would supply them, 15--16 of 16 subjects still improve. Within that representation, class-weighting the
episodic loss helps full MAML (+1.9--2.9 points) but not head-only ANIL. A matched
comparison, however, changes the conclusion. An EEGNet pretrained on the source
subjects' raw epochs and applied with no calibration at all beats the best
principal-component meta-learner by about six points on both corpora (13--15 of 16
subjects), and adapting it from four trials, whether by meta-learning or by
fine-tuning, adds no significant gain. In these data, cross-subject pretraining on an adequate input representation, not the meta-learning objective, carries few-shot ErrP personalization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A user who cannot speak or move spells a word letter by letter through a
brain-computer interface (BCI), and the speller picks the wrong letter. Their brain
has already registered the mistake: within roughly 250\,ms of the erroneous feedback
the EEG carries a stereotyped \emph{error-related potential} (ErrP), a fronto-central
negativity followed by a positivity near 320\,ms~\cite{chavarriaga2010learning}. A
decoder that reads that signal lets the interface undo its own mistake, with no extra
effort from the user~\cite{iturrate2015teaching}.

Calibration is what stands in the way. ErrP morphology shifts with anatomy,
electrode placement, and signal quality, so a decoder trained on one person transfers
poorly to the next, and every new user usually sits through tens of minutes of
labeled trials first, a large part of why ErrP-BCIs rarely leave the
lab~\cite{yasemin2023single}. Meta-learning offers a way around it: a model trained
across many users learns an initialization that adapts to a new user from a handful
of trials~\cite{finn2017model}. Whether that works for single-trial ErrP decoding,
and against which alternatives, is the question we answer.

\textbf{Novelty and objective.} Meta-learning for EEG has targeted mostly motor
imagery~\cite{han2024metaeeg,wang2025tcpl,wei2024harnessing}, while ErrP decoding has
advanced through deeper supervised or transfer models trained on substantial labeled data~\cite{ren2024deep,yasemin2023single} or through online reinforcement
learning~\cite{fidencio2025hybrid}. To our knowledge, no prior work tests few-trial
ErrP personalization across more than one paradigm with subject-level statistics.
Our objective is not a new algorithm but a controlled answer to whether, and why, meta-learning helps. We contribute:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{a four-trial ErrP personalization benchmark} of six meta-learning
methods against source-aware baselines and matched controls under one
leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) protocol, replicated on a second paradigm: meta-learners
beat a from-scratch decoder by 5--7 (INRIA) and 15--16 points (coadaptation), also
when calibration is realistic (unbalanced, consecutive trials, single draws);
\item \textbf{a matched analysis of input representation and baseline strength},
showing that an unadapted source-pretrained EEGNet on raw epochs outperforms the
principal-component meta-learners and that four-trial adaptation adds nothing
significant to it; and
\item \textbf{ablations and practical measures}: class-weighting the episodic loss
helps full MAML but not head-only ANIL, and we report calibration burden,
error-correction rates, and compute cost.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{ErrP detection.} ErrPs serve both to correct BCI output and as reward signals
for autonomous agents~\cite{chavarriaga2010learning,iturrate2015teaching}. Single-trial
detection remains hard; surveys of linear, kernel, and Riemannian classifiers point to
preprocessing and heavy class imbalance as the recurring issues~\cite{yasemin2023single},
and recent work adds physiological covariates~\cite{park2025improving}. EEGNet is a widely used compact neural decoder~\cite{lawhern2018eegnet}.

\textbf{Cross-subject transfer.} Beyond meta-learning, a major line is domain adaptation: Riemannian and Euclidean alignment before a shared
classifier~\cite{zanini2018transfer,wu2025revisiting}, and, for ERPs, minimum distance
to (weighted) class means on xDAWN super-trial
covariances~\cite{barachant2014plugplay,khazem2021minimizing}. EEG foundation models
pursue calibration-free use at far greater data and compute
cost~\cite{kuruppu2025foundation}.

\textbf{Meta-learning.} MAML learns an initialization that adapts in a few gradient
steps~\cite{finn2017model}; ANIL adapts only the head~\cite{raghu2020rapid}; Reptile
uses first-order updates~\cite{nichol2018first}; Prototypical~\cite{snell2017prototypical}
and Matching networks~\cite{vinyals2016matching} classify by similarity in a learned
embedding; FiLM conditions a network on side information~\cite{perez2018film}. For ErrPs, a recent CNN--transformer with transfer learning reports $71.8\%$ leave-one-subject-out and $78.7\%$ within-session accuracy~\cite{ren2024deep}; few-trial personalization has received far less attention.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Problem formulation}
A decoder is prepared on $N{-}1$ source subjects and specialized to a new user from
$K$ of that user's own trials; under LOSO each subject is the new user once and never
contributes to the model tested on it. ErrP data is imbalanced (error prior
$\sim$0.32 INRIA, $\sim$0.28 coadaptation), so the main benchmark draws a
\emph{class-balanced} calibration set of $\lfloor K/2\rfloor$ trials per class; the
regime of interest is $K{=}4$ (two error, two correct trials), and we sweep
$K\in\{4,10,20\}$. Sec.~\ref{sec:matched} relaxes the balancing.

\subsection{Personalization pipeline}
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline: each corpus is band-pass filtered,
epoched, reduced to 32 whitened principal components, and turned into class-balanced
episodes; a meta-model is trained across the source subjects under a class-weighted
episodic loss and adapted to the held-out subject in five gradient steps from its $K$
calibration trials.

\subsection{Datasets and preprocessing}
The \textbf{primary} corpus is the INRIA BCI Challenge (NER~2015) P300 speller (16
subjects, feedback-locked ErrP)~\cite{perrin2012objective}; the \textbf{second} is
ErrP-Coadaptation~\cite{ehrlich2018human} (all 16 released subjects, S03--S18), an
observation-ErrP paradigm in which a participant infers a robot's intended object
from its gaze. They differ in task, montage, and sampling rate (200 vs.\ 250\,Hz);
we run LOSO \emph{separately} on each and never train on one and test on the other.
A 4th-order zero-phase Butterworth band-pass (1--40\,Hz; plus a 50\,Hz notch at
250\,Hz) precedes epoching; epochs span $[-200,600]$\,ms around feedback, are
baseline-corrected over $[-200,0]$\,ms, and are rejected above $\pm100\,\mu$V. The
meta-learners use 32 whitened principal components of the flattened waveform (PCA fit
per fold on training subjects only); EEGNet and the covariance methods use raw epochs.
Sec.~\ref{sec:matched} tests what this asymmetry costs.

\subsection{Methods, baselines, and controls}
Six \textbf{personalization methods} run in the pipeline: Full-MAML~\cite{finn2017model},
MAML-ANIL~\cite{raghu2020rapid}, and Reptile~\cite{nichol2018first} (gradient-based);
Prototypical~\cite{snell2017prototypical} and Matching~\cite{vinyals2016matching}
networks (metric-based); and \textbf{SubjectConditioned}, a FiLM
encoder~\cite{perez2018film} that computes error/correct prototypes from the support
set and modulates a feature extractor. All episodic losses are class-weighted by
inverse class frequency.

\textbf{From-scratch reference.} Supervised, a three-layer MLP (128-64-2, LayerNorm,
GELU, dropout 0.3) trained on the $K$ trials alone, the only method that never sees
source data. \textbf{Matched controls} see the same source subjects with the same class
weighting as the meta-learners: Pretrain-FT freezes a source-pretrained MLP and fits a
logistic-regression probe on the $K$ trials; Pretrain-ZeroShot keeps its source head
and does not adapt. \textbf{Source-aware baselines}: EEGNet~\cite{lawhern2018eegnet}
pretrained on the source subjects (class-weighted cross-entropy), used frozen with an
$\ell_2$ logistic probe on the $K$ support embeddings, as in the main benchmark; and
xDAWN/tangent-space Riemannian~\cite{barachant2012multiclass} and covariance-alignment
classifiers~\cite{zanini2018transfer} whose LDA is fit on the $K$ trials.
Sec.~\ref{sec:matched} adds the raw-input variants of Table~\ref{tab:methods}.

\subsection{Implementation details}
MAML uses inner LR 0.01 (5 steps) and outer LR $5{\times}10^{-4}$ (Adam, cosine
decay, gradient clipping at 1); Full-MAML is second-order, ANIL adapts only the head
(first-order). Reptile uses 5 inner SGD steps and outer step 0.1.
SubjectConditioned derives FiLM $\gamma/\beta$ from a 32-dim subject embedding.
Meta-training runs 2000 iterations (meta-batch 4 subjects; episodes of 10 support +
40 query, class-balanced). EEGNet ($F_1{=}8$, $D{=}2$, $F_2{=}16$, temporal kernel
$f_s/2$) is pretrained with Adam ($10^{-3}$) and early stopping; at $K{<}10$ its probe
additionally sees three mixup interpolations per support trial. These hyperparameters are based on the cited papers' defaults and were fixed on INRIA before the
LOSO evaluation, using one pilot subject; they were then applied unchanged to the
coadaptation corpus, which thus checks against dataset-specific tuning. The
matched-benchmark settings were fixed a priori and never tuned: EEGNet-FT fine-tunes
all layers (Adam $10^{-4}$, 50 steps, BatchNorm statistics frozen); ANIL-/Reptile-EEGNet
reuse the MAML/Reptile settings on an EEGNet backbone; MDWM uses trade-off 0.5 on
4-filter xDAWN super-trial covariances. Code and per-fold results are released:
\url{https://github.com/Md-Shahidul-Islam-Dipu/Errp-Meta}.

\subsection{Protocol and statistics}
The main benchmark uses three seeds (42, 123, 456) and averages the episodic methods
and EEGNet over 10 calibration draws (other baselines: one draw). The unit of
inference is the \emph{subject}: we average each subject over seeds and draws and run
paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests at $N{=}16$ (exact null; floor
$p{=}3.1{\times}10^{-5}$), with Benjamini--Hochberg FDR correction within each family
of comparisons (69 per dataset in the main benchmark) and rank-biserial effect size
$r$. For headline pairs we give the median paired difference with a 95\% bootstrap
CI over subjects. Table $\pm$ values are across-seed spread. We report balanced
accuracy and AUROC, both robust to imbalance.

\section{Matched benchmark}
\label{sec:matched}
The main benchmark leaves three confounds: meta-learners and EEGNet saw different
inputs, EEGNet was only probed, and balanced, draw-averaged calibration is not what a
user supplies. We therefore retrained the gradient meta-learners, the FiLM encoder, the controls, and EEGNet once per fold (seed 42), added the variants marked in Table~\ref{tab:methods}, and scored all of them on \emph{identical} calibration draws under three protocols:
\emph{balanced} (as above), \emph{natural} ($K$ random trials, errors at the user's own
rate), and \emph{contiguous} ($K$ consecutive trials; draw 0 is the first $K$ trials
of the recording). Each method is scored on all remaining trials of the subject. When
a draw holds a single class, gradient methods adapt on it, while probe and
class-mean methods fall back to their source model. Table~\ref{tab:matched} closely reproduces the main benchmark (Reptile 0.593 vs.\ 0.594 on INRIA, 0.731 vs.\ 0.720 on coadaptation); remaining differences, e.g.\ for EEGNet (probe), reflect the query set (all remaining trials rather than a class-balanced subset).

More consequentially, the source-pretrained EEGNet \emph{with no calibration at all}
(EEGNet-ZeroShot) beats the PCA meta-learners; over the best, Reptile, by $+6.4$ points on INRIA
(13/16 subjects, BH-FDR $p=0.005$) and $+6.2$ on coadaptation (15/16, $p<0.001$). The
main benchmark's EEGNet looked weaker only because its four-trial probe discards the
source classifier (EEGNet-FT beats it by 7.2 and 13.9 points, 16/16). Adapting the
strong source model from four trials adds nothing significant: neither ANIL-EEGNet
($+1.4$, $-0.4$ points; BH-FDR $p=0.22$, $0.50$) nor full fine-tuning ($-0.8$, $+0.3$;
uncorrected $p\ge0.38$) beats EEGNet-ZeroShot at $K{=}4$; fine-tuning helps only marginally by $K{=}20$ ($+0.9$ points on coadaptation, uncorrected $p{=}0.004$). Raw input alone is not
sufficient either: first-order Reptile on the EEGNet backbone does no better than on PCA.
The Riemannian comparators do not beat Reptile on INRIA (BH-FDR $p\ge0.44$) and trail it by
9--12 points on coadaptation (16/16).

\textbf{Realistic calibration.} Relaxing the balancing leaves the ranking intact.
Under the contiguous protocol Reptile still beats Supervised by 6.8 (INRIA, 15/16,
$p=2{\times}10^{-4}$) and 19.3 points (coadaptation, 16/16, $p=3.1{\times}10^{-5}$), and
on the \emph{same} draw it wins 84\% and 100\% of the time; its worst-decile draw
(0.50, 0.64) is at or above Supervised's (0.48, 0.49). Unbalanced draws matter:
28--36\% of four-trial natural or contiguous draws contain no error trial at all.
Measured on the recordings, collecting two error and two correct trials takes a median
of 6 (INRIA) and 7 (coadaptation) raw trials, and 11 and 16 to cover 90\% of starting
points (five of each: medians 14 and 18.5).

\textbf{Practical significance and cost.} At the default decision threshold and the
coadaptation error rate (28\%), per 100 selections EEGNet-ZeroShot catches 22.2 of
28.4 errors while flagging 14.3 correct selections, Reptile catches 20.3 and flags 18.4,
and Supervised catches 15.9 and flags 32.7. On INRIA (32\% errors) every method flags
about as many correct selections as it catches errors (best: ANIL-EEGNet, 18.8 caught,
17.2 flagged), so none is yet useful for automatic undo there. Offline, one LOSO fold
(15 source subjects) costs 1.0--1.8\,min of Tesla T4 time for EEGNet pretraining,
1.4--2.6\,min for ANIL, Reptile, ANIL-EEGNet and the FiLM encoder, and 4.7--5.1\,min for
second-order Full-MAML; the full matched benchmark took 4.4 and 4.8 GPU-hours.
Per-user adaptation takes 5--17\,ms for the meta-learners, about 0.2\,s for EEGNet
fine-tuning, and none for EEGNet-ZeroShot.

\section{Discussion}
Within a fixed PCA representation, meta-learning is effective: it beats a
from-scratch decoder and controls matched on data access and class weighting, and
class-weighting the episodic loss helps its full-network variant. It does not, however, beat a
conventionally pretrained CNN that sees the raw epochs. Once that model's source head
is kept rather than replaced by a four-trial probe, it is the strongest calibration-free
decoder we tested, and four trials of adaptation, meta-learned or fine-tuned, do not
improve it. What carries few-shot ErrP personalization in our data is cross-subject
pretraining on an adequate input representation.

\textbf{Clinical implications.} In the main benchmark the meta-learners' edge over EEGNet was confined to four trials and closed by ten; the matched benchmark shows that this edge reflected the weak probe baseline rather than an advantage of meta-learning. The practical message is nonetheless favorable: a source-pretrained decoder is usable from the first trial of a session, which matters for users who tire quickly, and short per-session recalibration adds little. Where accuracy is still too low for automatic undo (INRIA), progress is more likely to come from choosing the operating point and from better source models than from faster adaptation. A meta-learned initialization remains a reasonable choice when only compressed features are available.

\textbf{Comparison with existing solutions.} A recent ErrP decoder reports roughly 72--79\% accuracy across leave-one-subject-out and within-session settings~\cite{ren2024deep}, and the single-trial classifiers surveyed in~\cite{yasemin2023single} fall in a comparable band; our calibration-free source EEGNet reaches 0.79 balanced accuracy on coadaptation and 0.66 on the harder INRIA speller. The comparison is indicative only: datasets differ, and balanced accuracy is stricter than raw accuracy.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i)~The matched benchmark uses one seed; the main benchmark's
seed spread ($\le$0.013) suggests this is minor. (ii)~The added EEGNet variants were
not tuned, so they are, if anything, understated. (iii)~Results are at the default
threshold; error-recall at low false-alarm rates is not analyzed. (iv)~All experiments
are offline, on two datasets, with calibration and test trials from the same
recording. (v)~The pipeline's hyperparameters were fixed with one INRIA pilot subject;
the unchanged replication on coadaptation limits, but does not exclude, dataset-specific
tuning.

% ===================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
We asked whether meta-learning can personalize an ErrP decoder from four calibration
trials, on two paradigms with subject-level statistics. Meta-learners on
principal-component features beat a from-scratch decoder and matched transfer controls,
robustly and under realistic calibration, and class-weighting the episodic loss helps
full MAML. A matched comparison shows that a source-pretrained EEGNet on raw
epochs, with no calibration, beats them on both paradigms and is not improved by
four-trial adaptation. Future work should combine strong raw-input source models with
operating-point selection and validate them in closed-loop online use.
